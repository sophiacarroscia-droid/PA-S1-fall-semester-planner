1. Absorption & Distribution Basics
ConceptWhat It MeansHow Drugs MoveKey Numbers / FactorsPEARLS
Therapeutic Index / Window The gap between the therapeutic concentration and the toxic concentration. A small window = little room for error before toxicity. A large window = more forgiving dosing. —
  • Narrow TI drugs need level monitoring (think vancomycin, aminoglycosides later in this guide).
Passive Diffusion High → low concentration, no energy needed. This is the primary way drugs are absorbed AND distributed throughout the body. —
  • Default answer if a question just says "how does the drug get into the cell/blood."
Active Transport A substrate carries the drug across the membrane. Energy required, and it's saturable — only so much substrate to go around. —
  • "Saturable" is the buzzword that tells you it's active transport, not passive diffusion.
Bioavailability The % of a dose that actually reaches systemic circulation. Affected by: first-pass metabolism, drug solubility, drug stability. IV = 100% bioavailable by definition.
  • Prodrugs are designed specifically to increase bioavailability.
First-Pass Metabolism Oral drugs absorbed via the GI tract go straight through the portal circulation → liver before ever reaching the rest of the body. Can be highly metabolized before the drug ever "counts." —
  • This is exactly why some drugs need much higher oral doses than IV doses.
Factors Affecting Distribution Lipid solubility, blood flow, binding to plasma proteins, drug structure. More lipid-soluble + more blood flow to a tissue = drug gets there faster/more. —
  • Highly protein-bound drugs = only the free/unbound fraction is active.
Volume of Distribution (Vd) A hypothetical number describing the volume of fluid a drug appears to be distributed through. Larger Vd = more distribution out into the tissues (drug is "hiding" outside the blood). —
  • Major storage site for drugs = adipose tissue.
2. Metabolism & Elimination
ConceptWhat It MeansWhere / HowSpecial NotesPEARLS
Major Site of Metabolism Liver CYP450 enzyme system does most of the work. CYP450 can be inhibited or induced (sped up) by certain drugs → this is the #1 mechanism behind drug interactions.
  • Drug A inhibits metabolism of Drug B → ↑ levels of Drug B. This exact logic pattern repeats on the exam.
  • Genetics affect CYP450 — yes. Most people are normal or rapid metabolizers.
First-Order Kinetics Rate of metabolism is proportional to drug concentration. A constant fraction (%) of the drug is metabolized per unit time. Most drugs work this way — including most elimination.
  • "% metabolized" language in a question = first order.
Zero-Order Kinetics Rate of metabolism is constant over time, regardless of concentration. A constant amount is metabolized per unit time — this is non-linear kinetics. Classic exam example: alcohol, phenytoin at high doses.
  • "Amount" (not %) metabolized per unit time = zero order.
Prodrug Administered inactive, then activated through liver metabolism. Designed this way specifically to increase bioavailability. —
  • If a drug "needs the liver to work," think prodrug.
Major Site of Elimination Kidneys Other elimination routes: lungs, urine, bile, sweat, saliva, tears, milk, stool. Elimination usually follows first order kinetics.
  • Creatinine clearance = estimated renal clearance (i.e., estimated GFR).
  • Estimate renal clearance using the Cockcroft-Gault formula.
3. Half-Life, Steady State & Loading Dose
ConceptDefinitionRelationship / DirectionDoes It Change Time to Steady State?PEARLS
Half-Life (t½) Time required to eliminate 50% of the drug in the body. ↑ renal clearance → ↓ t½  |  ↓ renal clearance → ↑ t½ —
  • Renal failure patients will have a longer half-life for renally-cleared drugs — this is a very testable "next step: adjust the dose" trap.
Time to Eliminate a Drug It takes 4–5 half-lives to essentially clear a drug from the body. — —
  • Same "4–5" number is used for both clearing AND reaching steady state — easy to mix up, so memorize both together.
Time to Reach Steady State Also 4–5 half-lives. Rate of infusion does NOT affect time to steady state.
Dose does NOT affect time to steady state.		 Steady state timing depends on half-life only, not on how much or how fast you give the drug.
  • This is a classic distractor trap: "increase the infusion rate to reach steady state faster" is wrong.
Loading Dose A higher dose given at treatment initiation. Still takes the usual 4–5 half-lives to reach true steady state, but gets the initial concentration closer to the eventual steady-state level faster. Decreases time to therapeutic effect (not time to true steady state).
  • Loading dose = "get to a working level fast," not "get to steady state fast." Know the distinction — it's a classic exam trap.
4. Antibiotic Pharmacodynamics & Resistance
ConceptDefinitionGoal / TargetExamplesPEARLS
Time-Dependent Antibiotics Efficacy correlates with time above the MIC, not peak concentration. Goal: stay above MIC for ~60% of the dosing interval. Once concentration exceeds 4x the MIC, you get no additional killing benefit (not a linear relationship). Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins), vancomycin.
  • Think "frequent dosing / continuous exposure" for this class.
Concentration-Dependent Antibiotics Efficacy correlates with the peak concentration. Optimal when peak is 10x the MIC. Aminoglycosides.
  • Think "one big dose, high peak" for this class — opposite strategy from time-dependent drugs.
Post-Antibiotic Effect Continued killing of the organism even after antibiotic concentration falls below the MIC. — —
  • This is part of why some antibiotics can be dosed less frequently than their half-life alone would suggest.
Mechanisms of Bacterial Resistance Three big buckets:
  • Prevention of the antibiotic from reaching its target site
  • Alteration of the target site
  • Inactivation of the antibiotic (e.g., beta-lactamase enzymes)
 Beta-lactamase production is the classic "inactivation" example — see the Penicillins chart.
  • If a question mentions an enzyme "chewing up" the drug, that's inactivation.

6. Penicillins & Beta-Lactamase Inhibitor Combos
MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageClass / NotesPEARLS
Beta-Lactams — General (all penicillins & cephalosporins) MOAInhibit cell wall synthesis by binding penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) → cell lysis. Bactericidal. Only active against actively growing organisms that make a peptidoglycan cell wall. Inactive against fungi and mycobacteria (no cell wall to attack).
  • The 4 beta-lactam classes: penicillin, cephalosporin, monobactam, carbapenem.
Antistaphylococcal Penicillins Oxacillin, Nafcillin, Cloxacillin, Dicloxacillin MOASame beta-lactam cell-wall mechanism, built to resist staph's penicillinase enzyme. CoverageActive against penicillinase-producing Staph aureus. Natural penicillins (basic).
  • These are your "MSSA" drugs — the ones that beat plain penicillin's biggest weakness.
Amoxicillin/Clavulanic Acid Augmentin MOAClavulanic acid is a beta-lactamase inhibitor added to protect amoxicillin. CoverageBeta-lactamase producing H. influenzae, N. gonorrhoeae, E. coli, Klebsiella, staphylococcal species. Aminopenicillin + BLI
  • BLIs "resemble beta-lactams but have no real antimicrobial activity of their own" — suicide inhibition: the beta-lactamase enzyme preferentially attacks the clavulanic acid/sulbactam instead of the real antibiotic, protecting it.
Ampicillin/Sulbactam Unasyn MOASulbactam = beta-lactamase inhibitor, same suicide-inhibition mechanism as above. CoverageBeta-lactamase producing Staph aureus, H. influenzae, Bacteroides fragilis (anaerobe), and most strains of E. coli. Aminopenicillin + BLI
  • Bacteroides fragilis coverage is the distinguishing feature vs. Augmentin on this list.
Piperacillin/Tazobactam Zosyn MOABeta-lactam + beta-lactamase inhibitor combo. ClassAntipseudomonal penicillin. Broad gram-negative coverage including Pseudomonas.
  • "Zosyn" = the go-to broad-spectrum hospital combo — remember it as the antipseudomonal penicillin.
7. Cephalosporins by Generation
Med / GenerationMOA / UseIndication & CoverageADE, CIs and Cross-ReactivityPEARLS
1st Gen Cephalexin (Keflex) UseCommon oral 1st-gen cephalosporin. CoverageUsed for cellulitis. —
  • Cefazolin = DOC for surgical prophylaxis (also 1st gen, IV).
2nd Gen Cefoxitin, Cefotetan UseAnaerobic coverage added compared to 1st gen. CoverageCover anaerobes (Bacteroides) — the key differentiator for this generation. —
  • If a question mentions Bacteroides + a "cef" drug, think 2nd generation.
Oral 3rd Gen Cefpodoxime (Vantin), Cefdinir (Omnicef) UseCommunity-acquired infections, oral outpatient use. CoverageOtitis media, pharyngitis/tonsillitis, sinusitis, bronchitis, pneumonia. —
  • These are your "everyday outpatient" oral 3rd-gens.
IV 3rd Gen Cefotaxime, Ceftriaxone UseEmpiric hospital therapy, broad gram-negative coverage. CoverageEmpiric treatment of meningitis in hospitalized patients. Also used empirically for community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) — combined with azithromycin to cover atypicals. Ceftriaxone alone is the DOC for gonorrhea. —
  • Cefotaxime/Ceftriaxone + azithromycin is a very testable combo — the "why azithromycin" is atypical coverage (Legionella, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia).
Antipseudomonal 3rd/4th Gen Ceftazidime, Cefoperazone, Cefepime UseBroadest gram-negative coverage in this class. CoverageActive against Pseudomonas. Used for hospital-acquired pneumonia. Cefepime is used empirically for febrile neutropenia — add vancomycin if MRSA is a concern. —
  • Cefepime + vanco = classic febrile neutropenia combo when MRSA is suspected.
Class-Wide Allergy Notes — — CIsAvoid all beta-lactams in patients with a history of immediate or accelerated reactions to penicillins or cephalosporins.
  • Cross-reactivity risk between a penicillin and a cephalosporin depends on how similar their side chains are — not a blanket "all PCN-allergic patients react to all cephalosporins" rule.
8. Carbapenems & Monobactam
MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageNotable GapsPEARLS
Imipenem/Cilastatin Primaxin & Meropenem Merrem ClassCarbapenems — the broadest spectrum of all beta-lactams. CoverageCover gram-positive, gram-negative, AND anaerobes — essentially everything. None major — this is the "big gun" class.
  • Think "last-line, covers almost everything" whenever you see these two names.
Ertapenem Invanz ClassCarbapenem, but narrower. CoverageBroad, but not as broad as imipenem/meropenem. Does NOT cover Pseudomonas — the single most important distinguishing fact.
  • Exam trap: picking ertapenem for a Pseudomonas infection. Don't.
Aztreonam ClassThe only monobactam antibiotic. CoverageGram-negative only (including Pseudomonas) — no gram-positive, no anaerobic coverage. No gram-positive or anaerobic activity.
  • Useful in patients with a true penicillin allergy who still need gram-negative coverage — low cross-reactivity with other beta-lactams.

10. MRSA/VRE Alternatives: Daptomycin & Linezolid
MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageADE, CIs and DDIsPEARLS
Daptomycin Cubicin UseAlternative to vancomycin. CoverageUsed for MRSA or VRE. ADEMyopathy — causes an increase in Creatinine Phosphokinase (CPK). Get a baseline CPK before starting.
  • CANNOT treat pneumonia — it binds to and is inactivated by pulmonary surfactant. This is one of the single most testable facts in this whole review.
Linezolid UseAlternative to vancomycin. CoverageUsed to treat VRE and MRSA. DDISerotonin syndrome (hypertension, hyperthermia, myoclonus, mental status changes) when combined with SSRIs (Zoloft, Prozac, Lexapro) or other serotonergic drugs.
  • Why: Linezolid is a reversible, nonselective MAOI — that's the mechanism behind the serotonin syndrome risk.
11. Tetracyclines
MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageADE, CIs and DDIsPEARLS
Doxycycline ClassTetracycline. CoverageCommunity-acquired pneumonia (especially if PCN allergy); nongonococcal urethritis (Chlamydia trachomatis); alternative agent for genital chlamydial infections; Community-Acquired MRSA skin infections. Also one of the DOCs for Lyme disease. See class-wide ADEs to the right — →
  • IV cephalosporin alternative for Lyme in the hospital: cefuroxime (flagged uncertain in the original review — worth confirming).
Tetracyclines — Class-Wide ChelationTetracyclines chelate cations — calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc. Chelation causes non-absorption of the drug. ADE
  • GI distress (dysphagia, odynophagia)
  • Phototoxicity — exaggerated sunburn reaction/rash after sun exposure
  • Vestibular problems: dizziness (especially minocycline)
  • Do not take with dairy, antacids, or iron supplements — this is the practical patient-counseling version of the chelation fact.
12. Aminoglycosides
MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageADE, CIs and MonitoringPEARLS
Aminoglycosides class SpectrumBroad spectrum of activity. UseIV aminoglycosides are almost always used in combination with another antibiotic, not alone. ADEOtotoxicity (hearing loss — may be permanent) and nephrotoxicity.
EliminationRenal excretion.
  • Must monitor serum levels for efficacy — this is a concentration-dependent class (see Section 4): the goal is a peak = 10x MIC.
13. Macrolides
MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageADE, CIs and DDIsPEARLS
Macrolides Erythromycin, Azithromycin, Clarithromycin MOABind the 50S ribosomal subunit → inhibit bacterial protein synthesis. Bacteriostatic. CoverageCommunity-acquired respiratory infections, sinusitis, otitis, pharyngitis/tonsillitis, prevention/treatment of MAC. Also cover the atypicals: Legionella, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia. ADEErythromycin: upper abdominal pain, bloating, nausea (worst GI offender of the three).
Serious ADEQT prolongation — class-wide cardiac risk.
  • Azithromycin 1g single dose = DOC for Chlamydia trachomatis.
  • Clarithromycin + PPI + amoxicillin = treatment for active duodenal ulcer from H. pylori.

Clindamycin — CoverageGram-positive and anaerobes — NOT gram-negatives. ADEMore strongly associated with C. diff colitis than other antibiotics — the single highest-yield fact about this drug.

MedMOA / UseIndication & CoverageADE, CIs and DDIsPEARLS
Sulfonamides — General MOACompete with PABA, preventing bacterial folate synthesis. — —
  • Folate-synthesis blockade is the mechanistic thread tying this whole class together.
